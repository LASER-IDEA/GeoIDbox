\begin{abstract}

Accurate \emph{vertical} positioning in low airspace is a critical challenge for the safety of urban air mobility and the integration of heterogeneous drone operations. While GNSS provides reliable horizontal coordinates, the \emph{vertical} channel suffers from severe discrepancies between barometric pressure altitude (used by manned aviation) and GNSS geometric altitude (used by UAS), which can exceed hundreds of meters in urban environments due to microclimate variations, urban canyon effects, and uncalibrated low-cost sensor biases. To address this measurement challenge, this paper proposes a novel edge-sensing framework comprising a custom multi-sensor IoT terminal and a physics-informed neural field algorithm. The proposed approach integrates physical atmospheric constraints with multi-resolution hash encoding, a physics-derived pressure bias formulation, and an altitude-aware curriculum learning strategy. This architecture explicitly decouples sensor-specific hardware noise from the environmental model, enabling zero-shot generalization to unseen, uncalibrated instruments. Evaluated on a high-fidelity urban dataset under a rigorous 8-fold leave-one-sensor-out cross-validation protocol, the proposed framework achieves a mean absolute error of 3.55 meters---representing a 90.4\% improvement over the uncalibrated physics baseline. The findings demonstrate that coupling purpose-built edge IoT instrumentation with physically constrained neural architectures can reliably provide a robust, real-time unified height reference for emerging low-altitude economies.

\end{abstract}

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}



Accurate and unified vertical positioning in VLL airspace is a foundational prerequisite for the safety of emerging UAM operations. To bridge the critical measurement gap between barometric pressure altitude and GNSS geometric altitude, this paper proposed a novel edge-sensing and spatial modeling framework. 
On the instrumentation front, we designed and deployed the GeoBox, a low-cost, multi-sensor IoT terminal capable of capturing synchronized, high-frequency atmospheric and geometric profiles in dense urban canyons. On the algorithmic front, we introduced a PINF architecture. By explicitly embedding the physical hypsometric equation as a deterministic baseline and utilizing multi-resolution hash encoding alongside a physics-derived pressure bias feature, the PINF effectively models the highly non-linear microclimate residuals and decouples uncalibrated sensor-specific noise that traditional analytical methods fail to capture. Furthermore, a 3-stage altitude-based Curriculum Learning strategy was implemented to ensure robust convergence across spatially heterogeneous data distributions. Evaluated through a rigorous LOSO metrological protocol on a real-world dataset collected in Shenzhen, China, the proposed framework achieved a MAE of 3.55 meters. This represents a substantial 90.4\% improvement over the uncalibrated physics baseline and demonstrates verified zero-shot generalization across unseen hardware under strict LOSO evaluation. The results conclusively demonstrate that coupling purpose-built edge IoT instrumentation with physically constrained neural architectures can reliably provide a meter-level unified height reference for heterogeneous U-space traffic.

\textbf{Limitations.} We acknowledge several limitations of the current work. First, all data was collected within a single urban area (${\sim}1\;\text{km}^2$ in Shenzhen), and the framework has not yet been validated across different cities with distinct microclimate regimes. Second, the dataset spans multiple days but not all four seasons, limiting our understanding of performance under extreme meteorological conditions (e.g., typhoons, severe temperature inversions). Third, all 8 GeoBox units employ the same barometer model (Murata ZPA4756); cross-model generalization to alternative pressure sensors remains untested. These limitations define concrete directions for future validation.

% \subsection{ Future Work}
Future research will focus on three directions. First, we plan extended multi-season, multi-city deployment campaigns to validate the framework across diverse meteorological and urban conditions. Second, we are developing GeoBox V2, featuring reduced weight compatible with UAV payloads, an alternative barometer model (Bosch BMP390) for cross-sensor validation, and an aerodynamic pressure compensation module for mobile deployment---enabling systematic trajectory-based altitude error evaluation. Third, we will explore meta-learning and few-shot adaptation techniques to enable rapid calibration to entirely new urban topologies with minimal initial sensor deployment, further enhancing the scalability of the GeoBox network for global UAM operations.
